The Wolf

Tune a circle of twelve strings by pure fifths and the last one will not close. The gap is the Pythagorean comma, about 23 cents, and it has to land somewhere. That place is the wolf: a fifth that howls. Every keyboard in history chose where to put it.

A string ripples at the rate it beats against its neighbour. A pure fifth is a straight line.

How to interact

  • Press on the rippling string and drag sideways, slowly. Slow drags turn the peg finely; fast ones turn it coarsely. Ripples thin out as the fifth comes true, and the tilt of the ripples tells sharp from flat.
  • Straight means pure. When a string is straight, the next one wakes. Tap a string you have reached to go back to it.
  • Keyboard: Left and Right arrows turn the peg, Shift for fine. Mouse wheel works too.
  • When the last string will not straighten, that is the wolf. It is not a bug.
  • Once the ring is closed, two sliders appear: one narrows every fifth together, the other moves the wolf along the ring. Dragging a string by hand afterwards undoes the sliders until you move one again. At equal temperament (the tick) the wolf has nothing to move, so its slider goes slack.

Notes

An idle page is nearly still: the one rippling string drifts slowly, and only your hand changes how many ripples it has. Nothing flashes: ripple changes are capped below three a second.

Calm mode is on, following your system's reduced-motion setting. Ripples no longer scroll, and they change density at once instead of easing.

The gearing of the drag is a hand-set choice, not a result. We could not test it on a real phone, and we cannot hear: this version has no sound. Ripple rates are computed from the tuning, not measured on an instrument, and the flicker limit is modelled, not tested on a screen. The sliders in the last step are the plainer half of the idea: they move the error, but they do not tell you which key you would play.
